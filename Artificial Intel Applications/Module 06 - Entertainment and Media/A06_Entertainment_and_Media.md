# Industry Overview

The Media and Entertainment industry focuses on the creation,
production, distribution, and consumption of content such as films,
television programs, music, video games, news, books, and digital media.
Its primary purpose is to inform, educate, and entertain audiences
through various platforms, including streaming services, social media,
television, radio, and theaters.

This industry serves a wide range of stakeholders, including content
creators, artists, production companies, advertisers, and consumers
worldwide. Advances in technology have expanded access to media,
allowing audiences to enjoy personalized content tailored to their
interests and preferences.

The Media and Entertainment industry is highly relevant today because
digital technologies and artificial intelligence are transforming how
content is created and delivered. AI is being used to improve content
recommendations, automate production processes, and generate new forms
of creative media. As the industry continues to evolve, it plays an
important role in shaping culture, communication, and public opinion
while also raising important ethical concerns related to misinformation,
copyright protection, and data privacy.

# Current AI Trends

The Media and Entertainment industry is undergoing rapid transformation
as generative AI moves from experimental novelty to core infrastructure
for storytelling. Three major trends illustrate this shift: AI-powered
content recommendations, agentic AI platforms built for studio
production, and AI-generated synthetic performances of real actors.

## AI-Powered Content Recommendations

Streaming platforms use AI algorithms to analyze viewing habits,
listening history, and user preferences. These systems deliver
personalized recommendations that increase engagement and customer
satisfaction. Netflix, for instance, estimates how likely a member is to
enjoy a given title by combining interactions with the service, such as
viewing history and ratings, the preferences of other members with
similar tastes, and information about the titles themselves, including
genre, categories, and actors. The system also factors in contextual
signals such as the time of day a member is watching, language
preferences, and the devices being used, and it continuously refines
itself based on ongoing viewing behavior, since titles a member engages
with more recently outweigh older activity in driving future
recommendations.

Spotify applies a similar approach to music discovery through features
like Smart Shuffle, which keeps listening sessions fresh with
personalized recommendations that match the vibe of an original
playlist, shuffling tracks and adding new, tailored suggestions. The
feature builds on Spotify\'s earlier Enhance tool, which allowed users
to discover new music while keeping their own track lists fresh, and
lets listeners see exactly which tracks were AI-recommended, since users
can identify these recommendations with a sparkle symbol next to a
track. Together, these examples show how Netflix and Spotify rely on
AI-driven recommendation engines to help users discover content that
matches their interests, reinforcing engagement across both video and
audio platforms.

## Agentic AI Platforms for Studio Production

A second trend is the rise of agentic AI platforms designed specifically
for studio production. Adobe\'s Firefly Foundry fine-tunes Firefly\'s AI
models on a studio or IP owner\'s own proprietary content, producing
high-fidelity images, video, audio, 3D, and vector outputs with a
complete understanding of a brand or franchise\'s creative universe.
Major talent agencies and production companies, including Creative
Artists Agency, United Talent Agency, and William Morris Endeavor, along
with directors such as David Ayer and Jaume Collet-Serra, have partnered
with Adobe specifically because the platform is designed to help the
media and entertainment industry move faster while preserving artistry,
authorship and ownership.

Building on this foundation, Adobe\'s new Firefly AI Assistant allows
creators to describe the outcome they want using their own words as the
assistant orchestrates and executes complex, multi-step workflows across
Adobe\'s Creative Cloud apps, including Photoshop, Premiere, and
Illustrator. Rather than operating as a single-purpose tool, Firefly now
functions as an all-in-one creative AI studio, integrating more than 30
third-party AI models alongside professional editing tools such as
studio-quality sound, advanced color adjustments and Adobe Stock
integration. Adobe has also announced it will extend this agentic
workflow to third-party AI models, including a collaboration with
Anthropic\'s Claude, signaling that conversational AI is becoming a
connective layer across the entire production pipeline rather than a
standalone feature.

## AI-Generated Synthetic Performances of Real Actors

A third trend is the use of AI to recreate the likeness and performance
of real actors, including those who have died, raising both creative
possibilities and ethical questions. In the film *As Deep as the Grave*,
director Coerte Voorhees used generative AI to complete the performance
of actor Val Kilmer, who had been cast in the role of Father Fintan
years earlier but died of cancer before he could film his scenes. With
the cooperation of Kilmer\'s estate and his daughter, Mercedes, the
production team built his AI likeness using younger images of Kilmer,
many of them provided by his family, and footage from his final years to
show his character in various stages of his life, and even used
Kilmer\'s voice, which, in his later life was damaged by a tracheal
procedure, to mirror the throat condition of the character he was
playing. While Kilmer\'s family has publicly supported the project, the
case has also drawn criticism over the ethics of digitally resurrecting
deceased performers for new roles they never agreed to film.

Together, these three trends show that AI\'s role in Media and
Entertainment now spans personalized content discovery, deeply
integrated production platforms, and increasingly controversial uses of
synthetic performance.

# Ethical Implications

## Deepfakes and Misinformation

One of the most pressing ethical concerns surrounding AI in media is its
capacity to produce deepfakes --- highly realistic fake videos, images,
and audio recordings that are difficult to distinguish from authentic
content. This capability raises serious ethical questions because
deepfakes can be used to spread misinformation, manipulate public
opinion, or damage a person\'s reputation, often without any clear
accountability for who created the content or why. As synthetic media
becomes more realistic, audiences are increasingly unable to tell real
content from fake content, which weakens public trust in what they see
online. This is not a hypothetical concern: during the recent conflict
between the United States and Iran, social media feeds, including my own
on Facebook, were flooded with images and videos claiming that Iranian
forces had struck or destroyed U.S. aircraft carriers. Fact-checkers
later found that there were no credible reports proving that happened,
and the lines on the vessel in the circulating video did not match those
in real pictures of the carrier. Some of this content was not even newly
generated; one widely shared clip first appeared online in June 2025
during an earlier conflict and appears to have been generated by a video
game, yet it resurfaced as supposed proof of a new attack. As analysts
have observed, these fabrications are becoming more convincing and
harder for even seasoned experts to identify, raising the ethical stakes
considerably: when fabricated war footage can circulate at scale and
shape public perception of real conflicts, the responsibility for
detecting and limiting that harm falls not only on individual users, but
on platforms, AI developers, and policymakers alike.

## Job Displacement and Workforce Impact

A second major ethical concern centers on job displacement and the
broader impact AI is having on the creative workforce. AI can now
automate tasks once handled by dedicated specialists, including video
editing, script writing, graphic design, voice acting, and content
moderation. While this increased efficiency benefits businesses and
consumers, it also raises legitimate concerns about reduced demand for
certain creative and technical roles, pushing workers to develop new
skills in order to remain competitive in an AI-driven industry. This
shift is not just an abstract industry trend; it\'s something playing
out in small businesses today. My wife, for example, runs a custom
apparel business where she works as the graphic designer, using Adobe\'s
AI tools to generate designs and logos for her customers. Before AI
tools like this existed, producing a single custom apparel order
typically required several distinct roles: a salesperson to work with
the customer, a graphic designer to create the artwork, a printer to
produce the graphics, and someone in production to press the design onto
the garment. Today, my wife handles nearly all of that herself. She
networks with other small businesses to find customers, uses Adobe AI to
generate the designs and logos, sends the files to a print shop, and
presses the final product herself. In effect, AI has allowed her to
consolidate three separate jobs into one, significantly lowering her
costs while keeping her business competitive. This example illustrates
both sides of the ethical tension: AI clearly increases efficiency and
lowers costs for small business owners, but it also reduces the number
of distinct roles needed to get the same work done, raising real
questions about what happens to the salespeople, designers, and
production workers whose tasks are increasingly consolidated or
automated altogether.

# Future Trends

Over the next decade, artificial intelligence is likely to fundamentally
reshape how movies, video games, and digital media are made and
consumed. Rather than simply assisting humans with editing tasks,
advanced AI systems will increasingly generate high-quality video, 3D
environments, and lifelike animation directly from text prompts. This
shift could allow viewers to interact with stories in real time,
adjusting plotlines or visual styles as they watch. Digital cloning may
also let actors star in international productions by matching their
voice and lip movements seamlessly to dozens of languages. Taken
together, these capabilities suggest the industry is moving away from
long, expensive filming schedules and toward faster, more automated, and
increasingly personalized digital experiences.

This technological shift also opens significant economic opportunities
for creators and media companies. Small, independent studios may gain
the ability to produce Hollywood-level visual effects on far smaller
budgets, shifting competitive advantage away from deep pockets and
toward creative talent itself. Established entertainment companies could
likewise breathe new life into older franchises by converting classic
archives into modern video games or localized formats automatically.
Advertising stands to be transformed as well: streaming platforms may be
able to insert different products into the background of a show
depending on exactly who is watching, creating new, highly targeted
revenue streams without interrupting the viewing experience.

At the same time, these advancements carry serious legal, economic, and
social risks. The growth of unauthorized deepfakes and the practice of
training AI models on copyrighted material is likely to fuel ongoing
legal battles over identity rights and ownership. On the economic side,
many traditional roles in visual effects, translation, and basic script
editing may shrink or disappear as automated tools take on more of this
work. There is also a risk of deeper cultural fragmentation: if AI
begins generating unique, hyper-personalized content for each individual
viewer, the shared cultural touchpoints and communal conversations that
have traditionally brought audiences together around common stories
could gradually fade.

# Your Reflections

What surprised me most while researching this topic was how quickly AI
moved from being a behind-the-scenes editing tool to something capable
of fully generating content, characters, and even digital recreations of
real people. I expected AI to streamline production, the way automation
has streamlined processes in my own work, but I didn\'t anticipate how
far synthetic media has already advanced, from AI-generated actors
completing a deceased performer\'s role in a feature film, to fabricated
war footage convincing enough to fool everyday social media users,
including myself when I saw the Iran/aircraft carrier images on my own
feed. That last point stood out to me the most. As someone who works
with data and automation systems professionally, I assumed I\'d be more
skeptical of obviously fake content, but the realism of these images
made it clear that even technically minded people can be fooled without
deliberately fact-checking what they see.

The example of my wife\'s apparel business also reframed how I think
about AI\'s impact. It\'s easy to discuss \"job displacement\"
abstractly, but watching AI let her personally absorb three roles that
used to require three separate people gave me a much more concrete sense
of both the opportunity and the disruption AI represents. It\'s
efficient and genuinely empowering for a small business owner, but it
also makes clear how quickly certain job categories could shrink
industry-wide if that pattern scales up.

As for whether I\'d want to work in this industry given these changes:
yes, but cautiously. The technical side of me finds the automation and
AI-driven production pipelines genuinely exciting, especially the idea
of agentic AI assistants orchestrating entire creative workflows,
similar to the automation and database work I already do at my own
company. I\'d be far more interested in roles that involve building,
managing, or overseeing these AI systems than in creative roles that are
most directly threatened by automation, like basic editing or script
revisions. Given how unsettled the legal and ethical questions still
are, particularly around consent, copyright, and deepfakes, I\'d want to
enter this field with realistic expectations about how unstable some of
these roles might be in the next five to ten years. The opportunity is
real, but so is the disruption.
