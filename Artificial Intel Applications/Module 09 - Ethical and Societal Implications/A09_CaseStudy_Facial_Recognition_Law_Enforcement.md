# **Introduction**

Artificial intelligence has moved from a specialized computing
discipline into a foundational layer of modern public infrastructure,
and few applications illustrate that shift as clearly as facial
recognition technology (FRT) in law enforcement. Police agencies now use
FRT to identify suspects, locate missing persons, and monitor public
spaces at a scale and speed no human analyst could match. Yet the same
capabilities that make FRT valuable to investigators have exposed
serious gaps in accuracy, oversight, and public accountability. This
paper examines FRT as a case study in AI ethics, reviewing how the
technology works, the benefits it offers, the ethical and societal
concerns it raises, and the risks that remain unresolved. It closes with
recommendations for governing FRT responsibly as its use continues to
expand.

# **Overview of the AI System**

Facial recognition technology identifies individuals by extracting
distinguishing features from an image and comparing them against
reference photos held in a database (Pew Research Center, 2022). Police
departments began experimenting with hand-coded facial matching in the
1960s, but modern systems rely on machine learning models capable of
searching millions of images in seconds (Pew Research Center, 2022).
Agencies deploy FRT through several channels: static comparisons against
mugshot and driver\'s license databases, real-time scanning through body
cameras and dashcams, and continuous monitoring of surveillance feeds at
events, transportation hubs, and border checkpoints (Davis & Hoss, P.C.,
n.d.). In practice, a match is meant to function as an investigative
lead rather than proof of identity, and officers are expected to
corroborate any result with additional evidence before making an arrest
(New Jersey State Bar Foundation, 2026).

# **Benefits and Positive Impact**

FRT\'s central advantage is speed: it allows investigators to screen
images against massive databases in a fraction of the time required by
manual review (Pew Research Center, 2022). Agencies use this speed to
generate leads in cold cases, verify identities at airports and border
crossings, and flag individuals with outstanding warrants during routine
patrols (Davis & Hoss, P.C., n.d.). The technology has also proven
useful outside criminal investigation -- national databases can match
missing children or trafficking victims against real-time surveillance
footage, and responders have used FRT to identify unconscious victims
during disasters (Davis & Hoss, P.C., n.d.). Retailers use similar tools
to flag repeat offenders, a meaningful counterweight to the more than
\$112 billion the retail industry loses annually to theft and fraud
(Davis & Hoss, P.C., n.d.). Public opinion reflects some of this
optimism: roughly three-quarters of Americans believe FRT helps police
solve crimes faster and locate missing people, and a plurality of 46%
view police use of the technology favorably overall (Pew Research
Center, 2022). Because these gains largely come from automating work
that would otherwise require substantial staff time, agencies tend to
frame FRT as an efficiency tool rather than a direct source of budget
savings.

# **Ethical Issues**

Three ethical concerns dominate the debate over FRT in policing.

## ***Racial and Gender Bias***

FRT systems inherit the biases of their training data. A widely cited
2018 MIT study found error rates as high as 35% when identifying
dark-skinned women, far above the error rate for lighter-skinned men
(New Jersey State Bar Foundation, 2026). Because many systems are
trained on historical suspect databases, they risk reinforcing patterns
of over-policing that already exist within the justice system, and
documented misidentifications have led to wrongful arrests of innocent
people, disproportionately people of color (New Jersey State Bar
Foundation, 2026).

## ***Privacy and Mass Surveillance***

Networked cameras and license-plate readers make continuous tracking of
people in public space possible, and courts are still deciding whether
this constitutes an unreasonable search under the Fourth Amendment
(Davis & Hoss, P.C., n.d.). Sixty-nine percent of Americans believe FRT
allows police to track anyone\'s location at any time, a concern that
has led several cities and states to restrict or ban police use of the
technology altogether (Pew Research Center, 2022; New Jersey State Bar
Foundation, 2026).

## ***Lack of Transparency***

Many FRT systems function as a "black box": even the officers using them
often cannot explain how a match score was generated. In one New Jersey
case, State v. Arteaga, prosecutors initially withheld the error-rate
data a defendant needed to challenge the reliability of an FRT match; an
appellate court ultimately ruled that such data must be disclosed (New
Jersey State Bar Foundation, 2026). Scholars have called for mandatory
data protection impact assessments to address this opacity (Almeida et
al., 2021).

# **Societal Implications**

## ***Workforce Impact***

FRT is designed to generate leads, not replace investigators, but it is
shifting police work from manual matching toward oversight of automated
systems. This shift requires human-machine collaboration, since flawed
algorithms produce real harm without a trained officer able to catch an
error, and it has already created new compliance roles, such as the Data
Protection Officer positions now required in the European Union (Almeida
et al., 2021).

## ***Inequality and Public Trust***

Sixty-six percent of Americans -- and 48% of Black adults specifically
-- expect police to use FRT to monitor Black and Hispanic neighborhoods
more heavily than others, so the technology risks deepening distrust in
communities already skeptical of policing (Pew Research Center, 2022).
Trust rises when safeguards exist: roughly two-thirds of the public say
they would find FRT more acceptable if officers were trained to
recognize system errors, and about half would support it if the public
were notified when a space is being scanned (Pew Research Center, 2022).

## ***Global and Regulatory Divide***

No single international standard governs FRT. The European Union\'s
General Data Protection Regulation requires data protection impact
assessments and has pushed some U.S. companies toward "privacy by
design" practices to retain access to European markets, while the United
States relies on a patchwork of state laws and lacks a dedicated federal
privacy regulator (Almeida et al., 2021). Acceptance of the technology
also varies by context: it is far more accepted at national borders,
where identity checks are already routine, than in public squares or
workplaces (Almeida et al., 2021).

# **Risks and Challenges**

## ***Technical Limitations***

Accuracy still depends heavily on image quality, lighting, and camera
angle, and even well-designed systems perform unevenly across
demographic groups (Almeida et al., 2021). The black-box nature of
matching algorithms compounds this problem, since officers frequently
cannot assess how confident a given match really is (Almeida et al.,
2021).

## ***Security Risks***

Because FRT processes sensitive biometric data at scale, weak access
controls can expose that data to misuse. Sweden\'s data protection
authority fined its national police roughly €250,000 after unauthorized
employees were found accessing FRT software (Almeida et al., 2021).

## ***Misuse and Abuse***

Companies such as Clearview AI have built databases of billions of faces
by scraping social media without consent, then sold access to that data
to government and private clients alike (Almeida et al., 2021). A 2023
federal watchdog review found that most law enforcement officers using
facial recognition received no training before being given access to the
tool, and several agencies could not fully account for how their staff
used it (Lipton & Guariglia, 2023). Combined with the bias and
surveillance concerns described above, this lack of training leaves
substantial room for wrongful arrests and unchecked monitoring of
protests and other constitutionally protected activity.

# **Recommendations**

Addressing these problems does not require abandoning FRT, but it does
require closing the gap between the technology\'s capability and the
oversight built around it.

First, mandatory training should precede any officer\'s access to FRT,
covering how the software generates a match, its documented error rates,
and its limitations across demographic groups; the absence of this
training, identified by federal auditors, is arguably the most
correctable failure in current practice (Lipton & Guariglia, 2023).
Second, FRT vendors should be required to publish independently audited
accuracy and bias statistics for their systems, and agencies should be
barred from procuring tools that have not undergone this testing. Third,
legislatures should establish clear rules for when FRT can be used,
requiring a documented basis before a search, notice to defendants when
a match contributes to an arrest, and disclosure of error-rate data
during any resulting prosecution, following the precedent set in State
v. Arteaga (New Jersey State Bar Foundation, 2026). Finally,
jurisdictions should require regular data protection impact assessments
and independent audits, modeled on the European Union\'s approach, so
that governance keeps pace with the technology as it evolves (Almeida et
al., 2021).

# **Conclusion**

Facial recognition technology gives law enforcement genuine advantages:
faster investigations, better tools for finding missing people, and
stronger security at high-risk sites. But those advantages come bundled
with documented racial and gender bias, expanding surveillance
capability, and a transparency gap that leaves even the officers who use
FRT unable to fully explain how it works. Public opinion reflects this
tension -- support for FRT rises sharply once training, notice, and
accountability measures are in place. Closing the gap between what FRT
can do and what oversight currently requires of it is the central task
facing lawmakers, vendors, and police departments alike. Handled well,
FRT can remain a valuable investigative tool; handled poorly, it risks
becoming exactly the unaccountable surveillance system its critics
already fear.
