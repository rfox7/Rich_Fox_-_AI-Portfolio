# **Introduction**

Government agencies are starting to use artificial intelligence (AI) to
speed things up. One of the clearest examples is hurricane forecasting.
For years, meteorologists have gotten pretty good at predicting where a
hurricane is headed. But predicting how strong it will get, especially
when it blows up in strength right before landfall, has been one of the
toughest problems in weather science. For people living on the Texas
Gulf Coast, that\'s not just a science problem. It\'s the difference
between prepping for a moderate storm and getting blindsided by a
monster one.

![](media/image2.jpeg){width="2.95in" height="1.9666666666666666in"}This
report looks at how NOAA and the National Hurricane Center (NHC) have
started using AI models to forecast hurricanes, especially through a
partnership with Google DeepMind during the 2025 Atlantic hurricane
season. It looks at what worked, what didn\'t, and uses Hurricane
Melissa as the main example. Then it proposes a new idea: taking this AI
forecasting a step further to give Gulf Coast communities more useful,
personalized storm warnings.

# **Case Study Analysis: AI in NOAA Hurricane Forecasting**

## **The Problem**

Hurricane track forecasts, meaning where a storm is going, have gotten
better and better over the decades. But intensity forecasts, meaning how
strong a storm gets, have not kept up. Rapid intensification is when a
storm\'s wind speed jumps by at least 35 miles per hour in just 24
hours. It\'s happening more often as ocean temperatures rise, and it\'s
dangerous because it can catch people off guard. Older weather models
work by simulating the physics of wind, moisture, and heat moving
through the atmosphere. They\'ve historically struggled to capture the
fine details inside a storm that drive these sudden jumps in strength.
That means less warning time for emergency managers and residents, which
is a big problem on the Gulf Coast, where warm water can fuel a storm
right up until it makes landfall.

## **AI Tools and Techniques Implemented**

During the 2025 season, NHC forecasters added AI guidance to their usual
lineup of models, which already includes NOAA\'s own Hurricane Analysis
and Forecast System (HAFS). The big addition was WeatherNext, a model
built by Google DeepMind and Google Research. Unlike the older models,
WeatherNext doesn\'t simulate atmospheric physics directly. Instead,
it\'s trained on decades of historical weather data plus data on extreme
past hurricanes, and it runs 50 different simulations at once to give
forecasters a range of likely outcomes instead of just one guess. At the
same time, NOAA rolled out its own new lineup of AI weather models,
which the agency says are faster, more accurate, and use way less
computing power than the old models. NOAA has also started using machine
learning to clean up radar data collected by its Hurricane Hunter
aircraft, pulling more usable data out of each flight.

## **Outcomes and Benefits**

-   Historic rapid-intensification call: WeatherNext correctly predicted
    Hurricane Melissa would blow up into a Category 5 storm five days
    before it happened. That was the first time NHC had ever forecast a
    storm reaching Category 5 starting from Category 1 wind speeds.

-   More warning time: Because forecasters trusted that early call,
    Jamaican officials got extra time to move resources and start
    evacuations before landfall. That\'s the whole point of better
    forecasting: more time to act.

-   AI came out on top: By the end of the season, NHC\'s own report
    found the AI models, led by WeatherNext, gave the best track and
    intensity forecasts of any single model that year.

-   Cheaper and faster: NOAA\'s new AI models produce forecasts using a
    lot less computing power than the old physics-based models, so
    guidance gets to forecasters and the public faster.

## **Challenges and Limitations**

-   Not every AI model is good: Some AI models flopped. One European AI
    model barely beat a coin flip on intensity because it was trained on
    data that tends to weaken storms too fast, giving it a built-in bias
    toward underestimating strength.

-   Trust takes time: NHC forecasters have said openly that it took a
    couple of full hurricane seasons of watching these models perform
    before they felt comfortable leaning on them. Part of the reason is
    that AI models are more of a black box than the older physics-based
    ones.

-   AI is a tool, not a replacement: Senior NHC forecasters are clear
    that no AI model nails every detail of a storm\'s track, strength,
    and structure. Human judgment and the older physics-based models
    still matter a lot.

-   Good forecasts aren\'t the whole story: Even a great intensity
    forecast doesn\'t automatically tell you what storm surge, wind
    damage, or flooding will look like on the ground. That still takes
    more modeling and local know-how.

Put together, this shows a forecasting system that\'s genuinely changing
for the better. AI has already delivered a historic, life-saving
improvement in predicting rapid intensification. At the same time,
forecasters are still working through real questions about how reliable
these models are, how much they can be trusted, and how they fit
alongside decades of physics-based forecasting experience.

# **Innovative Proposal: Hyperlocal AI Storm Alerts for Gulf Coast Communities**

## **The Issue**

The Hurricane Melissa case shows AI can now give forecasters days of
extra warning before a storm blows up in strength. But that better
regional forecast doesn\'t automatically reach individual households in
a way they can actually use. Gulf Coast communities, including plenty
along the Texas coast, are big, low-lying, and don\'t all have the same
resources. A county-wide evacuation map or a general hurricane watch
doesn\'t tell one specific family in one specific flood-prone
neighborhood how much time they really have or what they should do about
it. That\'s the gap this proposal is trying to close: getting a more
accurate national forecast down to the personal, local level people
actually need to act on it in time.

## **Proposed Application**

![](media/image4.jpeg){width="4.9in" height="3.2666666666666666in"}This
report proposes a system that sits downstream of NOAA\'s forecasting
work rather than trying to replace it: a hyperlocal AI storm-alert
platform. It would take in NHC and WeatherNext-style forecasts, plus
NOAA\'s flood-mapping data and local info like road capacity and shelter
locations, and turn all of that into a risk score for a specific
address. Instead of a generic county-wide bulletin, a resident would get
a plain-language alert telling them their storm-surge and wind risk,
roughly how much time they have before things get bad, and a suggested
evacuation route and shelter based on real-time road and shelter data.

Figure 1 shows how this would work. Storm data, including satellite,
radar, and NHC/AI model guidance, first goes through an AI step that
turns the regional forecast into local risk. Then a human
emergency-management reviewer checks that guidance before alerts
actually go out to the affected neighborhoods. Just like NOAA and NHC do
today, a person stays in the loop before anything is sent. That keeps
someone accountable for a decision this important, while still getting
the speed and precision AI adds.

## **Justification and Expected Outcomes**

This idea builds directly on what the case study shows works: AI\'s
ability to catch rapid intensification early. It also fixes something
the case study doesn\'t solve on its own, which is getting that extra
warning time down to the household level. Expected results include more
useful warnings for people in flood-prone or under-resourced
neighborhoods, evacuation timing based on a family\'s actual situation
instead of a countywide average, and multilingual, mobile-first alerts
that reach people broadcast news might miss. Because this platform
builds on top of NOAA\'s guidance instead of replacing it, it gets
better automatically as NOAA\'s own AI models improve.

## **Anticipated Challenges**

-   Pulling the data together: Combining NHC and AI forecasts with local
    road, shelter, and demographic data in real time during a
    fast-moving storm is a real technical challenge.

-   False alarms: Too many alerts and people start ignoring them. Too
    few and people don\'t get warned in time. Getting that balance right
    is critical.

-   Who gets left out: An app or text-based system could miss households
    without reliable phones or internet, which is exactly the kind of
    gap this platform is supposed to close.

-   Staying in sync: Any local alert would need to line up with, not
    contradict, official National Weather Service and county warnings,
    so people aren\'t getting mixed messages during a crisis.

# **Conclusion**

NOAA\'s 2025 hurricane season shows that AI can close one of the biggest
gaps in disaster forecasting: catching rapid intensification early
enough to matter. Hurricane Melissa is a clear example. An AI model gave
forecasters days of extra warning for a storm that ended up being one of
the strongest ever recorded. At the same time, the case study shows that
even a great forecast still needs human judgment, careful checking, and
backup from physics-based models, and that AI\'s benefits aren\'t spread
evenly across every model or every community yet. The hyperlocal alert
platform proposed here takes that unevenness on as the next problem to
fix, turning a better national forecast into personal, useful guidance
for the Gulf Coast households who need it most. As AI forecasting keeps
improving, the next challenge for public-sector AI isn\'t just
predicting the storm better. It\'s making sure that better prediction
actually reaches everyone in time to act on it.
